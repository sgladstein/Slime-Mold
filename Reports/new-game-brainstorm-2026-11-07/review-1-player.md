# Review 1: the player's hands and heart (2026-11-07)

*Reviewer 1 of 3. Judged as a designer of puzzle and action games: moment-to-moment feel, where the "aha" is, what a new player does in the first minute, and whether it is still fun on the tenth play.*

## 1. My top 5

1. **Arc with scars.** You call lightning by holding and releasing, and every bolt leaves a mark the next one follows. Arc, plus Fulgurite's glass trail, plus D's run.
2. **Firedamp, "the gas is your dynamite".** A's burn band and pooling gas, D's standing torch and run, played with placed tools rather than a card row.
3. **Mould, herded with a flashlight.** B's slime mould, with a lamp under your finger as the main verb.
4. **Fault.** Tap the ice and a crack runs. It is the best single touch-and-sound verb in the set.
5. **Quench, merged.** C's lid-and-drip engine, D's forge that needs *some* lava, and A's rule that boiled water comes back as rain. The cheapest honest test.

## 2. Combinations

**A. Arc + Fulgurite: the bolt builds the path for the next bolt.** *Strong; this is my number 1.* Arc's weak spot is that each strike is a one-off: a flash, and the world barely changes. Fulgurite's best idea is that a bolt leaves solid glass. Move it across. A bolt through soil or sand leaves a scorch or glass line that conducts a little for the rest of the level. Now every strike is also a building move: your third bolt runs down your first one's scar and forks off its end. On sand levels, keep Fulgurite's "pull the plug" as an end-of-level treat: the loose sand drains away and your glass tree stands up. Falling sand as a garnish, not the base, so it stays inside the owner's brief.

**B. Arc + Firedamp: a storm over gassy ground.** *Muddy as a core game; good as one level.* Use it as a boss storm inside Arc's run instead: gas seeps from a bog, and a bolt only sets it off where the gas is in the burn band.

**C. Mould absorbs Veins, and takes Lode's ending.** *Strong.* B already proposed that Mould absorb Veins, and I agree. Add one thing from Lode: the score lands as a quick tally after each dish, counting up loop by loop with a sound for each one. That replaces Lode's whole Balatro layer, which on its own is arithmetic with a simulation as decoration. Mould's "the storm cuts three tubes" finale is the best ending of all 21 concepts, because it tests the thing you built. Lode's tally makes that test feel like a payout.

**D. Quench + Floodgate + Landslide: one valley game about water that is never destroyed.** *Good merge, low ceiling.* All three are the same game: shipped water, lava, steam and rubble, and a town to keep dry. Keep three things:
- from A, the law that boiled water comes back as rain somewhere else;
- from C, the lid trick, where a roof turns one splash into a quench engine;
- from D, the forge that needs a trickle of lava, so blocking everything also loses.

Leave Landslide's charges to one card at most.

**E. Triggers belong on the map, in every concept.** A's Fuse ("goes off when the gas here can burn") and Mould's Buried sit visibly on the map, waiting for the world. That is far easier to follow than a trigger queued in a card strip.

## 3. Why each would be fun, and what might stop it

**1. Arc with scars.** It is the only concept where a single press gets a loud answer in under a second: a flash, a fork, a lamp lighting, a puddle bursting into steam. That is what the satchel never had. The aha is physical and clean: water pulls lightning, glass turns it away, and the bolt goes where *you* made it easy. The scars give the tenth play a life: a storm becomes one drawing you keep adding to, not a set of separate shots. D's run (one town, rising storms, copper kept between storms) gives you a reason to start another. **Biggest risk: the aim.** If the bolt is predictable, the game is "draw a line, watch it traced", which is dull. If it is random, it is dice. Everything depends on the band in between, and A's 70–95% hit test is the right way to find it. My fix (section 4) lets the player watch the bolt feel its way before committing.

**2. Firedamp.** It has the best emotional curve of the 21: dread you can see (haze creeping toward a lamp), then release (a blue ribbon racing back through gas you herded). "Too thick to burn, so thin it first" is learned from one fizzle, and every clever play after that is built on it. What none of the three versions quite says: the gas must be the player's **resource as well as the threat**. A bigger controlled bang should give more ore, so you are tempted to let a pocket grow thick beside the seam you want. Greed against safety is what keeps the tenth play tense. **Biggest risk:** pushing gas that spreads on its own feels mushy. If a Puff doesn't visibly bend the haze within half a second, the hand goes numb.

**3. Mould.** It has the most charm, the closest match to the owner's interests, and the best finale. The "it's alive" moment is real. It reaches out a fan, finds the oats, thickens one tube and pulls back the rest, and a player will say "it *decided*". Network quality (reach, thrift, toughness) is a deep puzzle with no formula to learn, so it lasts. **Biggest risk: watching.** Growth is slow, and cards dropped every 2 seconds onto something that answers in 4 or more is the ant lab's complaint over again. It needs a verb the mould answers instantly, even while the growth stays slow (section 4).

**4. Fault.** Tapping ice and hearing a crack run and branch is one of the most satisfying sensations a game can borrow; everyone has stepped on a frozen puddle. Meltwater then Frost is a real aha ("the water was the multicast"). Slabs come loose as blocks, cobbles and grit, which this engine already does well. It needs no cards: tap, drill and pour are direct verbs. **Biggest risk:** the tension field is invisible, so cracks look arbitrary. Structural collapse has surprised this project many times, and one wall failing without warning ends the level.

**5. Quench, merged.** It is almost all shipped engine. Playing the roof rather than the water is a lovely indirect verb, and the forge trickle gives a goal with a middle. **Biggest risk:** it is slow, calm and close to From Dust and Creeper World, and by the tenth play you know how the lid works. Treat it as a probe of whether engine chemistry makes combos fun, not as the game.

**Clever on paper, dull in the hand.**
- **Tincture:** everything goes brown, and stirring dye is calm.
- **Saltworks, Brine and Delta:** the main growth takes minutes to show.
- **Etch:** the patterns are famously hard to predict, so seeding them feels random.
- **Lode:** the fun is in the multiplier, and dice could replace the physics.
- **Veins:** a race you mostly watch.
- **Landslide:** one press, then chaos.

**High Tide** is genuinely fun, but it is a falling-sand game, which the owner set aside.

## 4. Extensions to my top 3

### Arc with scars

**The verb: hold to call, release to strike. No card row in the moment.**
- **Hold** on the cloud, and the leader starts creeping down. Slow it to 1–1.5 seconds. Faint feelers flicker ahead of it. They are the simulation's own candidate branches, so they are a preview the player can trust, not a hint someone drew.
- **Release**, and the brightest feeler becomes the bolt: a flash, a crack, a hit.
- **Hold too long**, and the cloud overflows by itself onto the tallest thing in the valley. Every strike asks the hand a real question: how long do I dare hold?
- **The hold is the modifier.** A short hold gives a weak, bushy bolt; a long hold gives a straight, heavy spear. That covers Bushy, Straight and Heavy with no words to read.
- **While the leader creeps, one tap places one thing**: a splash of water or a pinch of copper dust. The bolt bends toward it in front of you. That is steering in real time, and it is the move good players will show off.
- **Cards move to the run layer.** Between storms you pick one of three tools or rules (Rod, Glass, "bolts that hit water spread across it"). A fixed card order would fight the hold-and-release rhythm.

**Surprises the explorers missed.**
- **Side flash.** Copper that doesn't touch ground (water or wet soil) makes the bolt jump the last gap with an extra spark, sometimes onto a roof. It teaches "wire it to the ground" with no text.
- **Surface spread.** A bolt into a pond runs across the surface as a bright ring, and anything metal touching the water lights up.
- **Three fade speeds.** Ionised air lasts about 3 seconds, scorched soil the rest of the storm, and glass for good. That gives three time scales to plan with.
- **Fire draws the next strike** (B's rule), so a mistake becomes a choice: light the oil on purpose to pull the next bolt away from the village.

**The first 60 seconds.** The screen shows a cloud, a bell tower, one lamp on a hill, and the words "Hold to call the lightning."
1. The first hold: the feelers wander, the player lets go, and the bolt hits the tower because it is tallest. The bell clangs and a roof sparks. It is a lesson, not a loss.
2. One water splash is offered. On the next hold the player taps the hill, and the feelers swing toward the wet patch.
3. They release, and the lamp lights. "Water pulls lightning" lands by about 45 seconds.

**On a phone.** A bright bolt on a dark sky reads at any size, but a branch one cell wide does not: at 512 cells across a phone, a cell is smaller than a pixel. So:
- draw bolts 2–3 cells wide with a glow;
- make phone levels about 200–256 cells wide, or let the player zoom;
- let the hold work anywhere on the sky, not on a small button.

**A sharper first prototype (2 days, not 4).** Skip the charge field. Grow the bolt as a random walk biased toward the nearest conductor, using a plain distance map, and read each fine cell's material at the tip. Add hold and release with feelers, one splash, one tower and one lamp. The only question is whether hold, feel and release is fun in the hand. Publish a page with a paired GIF (no splash against one splash) and, beside it, the counts over 10 seeds: strikes, lamp hits, tower hits. Build the real field only if the feel works and the aim needs it.

### Firedamp

**The verb: placed tools, live doors, thrown matches. No card row.** Herding gas is continuous and real-time, and the gas will surprise you, so a fixed card order is friction, not depth. Give the player small stocks of:
- **fans**, dragged to aim, that blow until you pick them up;
- **doors**, tapped to slam or open (Lemmings-style live toggling, the most responsive verb available);
- **matches**, thrown;
- **fuse markers**, "lights when the gas here can burn", sitting on the map.

The Noita feel moves to the run: between levels you pick perks that flip a rule. D's "gas is heavier than air" and "each blast refills the vents" are exactly right.

**Make the gas the dynamite.** Ore comes only from blasts, and a blast's size follows the gas that was in it. So you *want* thick gas beside the seam and thin gas near the lamps, and every level becomes greed against safety.

**Surprises.**
- **Slam a door on a moving plume.** The gas piles up and thickens behind it: too thick to burn, safe for now, a bomb for later.
- **D's standing torch** becomes a cutter you aim with a fan.
- **Each bang opens new holes**, so the map is rewritten for the next gas.
- **Sound carries the band.** A thin pocket fizzles with a soft hiss; a burnable one goes "whump", deeper the thicker it was. Players will learn the band by ear.

**The first 60 seconds.**
1. One gallery, one leak, one lamp, one match. The gas pools green in the dip.
2. The player throws the match into the pool: a fizzle, and the words "too thick to burn".
3. They tap the fan, and the pool's edge turns yellow.
4. The next match sends a blue ribbon racing along that edge.

**On a phone,** the three fixed colours work only as strong colours over dark rock, never a faint tint. Doors need big tap targets.

**A cheaper first prototype (2 days).**
- Put the gas field on a hand-drawn tunnel mask.
- Have the fan add drift to the gas directly inside its cone, not through the air grid (see section 5).
- Add the burn band, the flame front, doors and matches. Skip the hand-off to the explosion code.

The question it tests: can a new player say where the flame will stop before they light it?

### Mould

**The verb: a flashlight under your finger.** The mould already flees light. Drag a lamp across the dish and the veil *recoils* from it live, within a fraction of a second. Growth can stay slow, because the recoil is the instant answer the hand needs. Oats become a scarce second verb (five per dish), and Snip a rare third. You herd with light and bribe with food. Cards move to between dishes, as in D's run; the same holds for Arc, Firedamp and Fault, because a fixed order of plays gets in the way of reacting when the world answers over seconds. Run each dish as a time-lapse, with a "wait" button that skips ahead while you aren't touching it.

**Surprises.**
- **A light that is a moving wall.** Pin the lamp over the trunk, and the tube thins and the flow visibly reroutes through a side loop. That previews the storm test.
- **Two moulds that fuse.** Their pulses fall into step, and the beat across the screen changes.
- **A storm finale you watch up close.** After each cut, the pulse finds its way round, or fails to. A planned network glows on. A tree-shaped one goes dark region by region.

**The first 60 seconds.**
1. A yellow blob and two oat piles.
2. It reaches out a fan, finds the near pile, and the fan collapses into one bright tube. The player sees it choose.
3. A far pile appears, with a lamp in between, and the player's finger is the lamp.

**A sharper prototype (2 days, not 4–5).** Skip the flow solve on a graph of junctions. The well-known agent model of slime mould (Jeff Jones, 2010) builds the same kind of network in seconds of real time:
- thousands of tiny walkers lay a trail that spreads and fades;
- each walker turns toward the strongest trail;
- it runs on exactly the spreading-and-fading field this engine already has.

Test whether herding with a flashlight feels alive. Pay for the proper thickening model only if the cheap networks aren't good enough to score.

## 5. Wrong or optimistic claims

I checked three claims, as the brief allows.

**1. "Arc is the cheapest to run; the field costs nothing while nobody casts" (A, B, D). Optimistic.**
- **What I checked:** the doc comment on `strike` in `src/sim/weather.rs`.
- **What it confirms:** today's lightning is only drawn and touches no cells, as D says.
- **What it also says:** the renderer **forces a full-screen redraw on every strike frame and the one after**. So every bolt already costs the dirty-rect skip for its flash.
- **What that means:** a game built on frequent strikes pays this on every bolt. That includes D's farm, where "one press sets off a dozen strikes", and B's Again chains. On top of that, the fire and steam a bolt starts keep chunks awake. Quote the `ascii` worst frame over a run of strikes, not over one cast.

**2. "Heat moves the air grid", which A's "breathing fire" combo (step 4) and its Warm card rely on. Probably false today.**
- **What I checked:** `src/sim/field.rs`. A had flagged this one "to check".
- **What is there:** the coarse grid carries pressure, wind, temperature and light. Its header says cells do not exchange heat with it; that note may be out of date.
- **What I did not find:** any term by which warm air pushes the wind upward. Hot gas *cells* rise, but the wind does not seem to be driven by heat.
- **What that means:** the breathing fire and Warm are new work.
- **A second problem in the same file:** the grid is **1/8 resolution**, one field cell per 8x8 block of world cells. Puff and Draw would push gas in 8-cell blocks. An Arc charge field "on the coarse grid" (A) could not feel a wet streak or copper smear one or two cells wide, so the bolt's tip must read the fine cell itself. B's quarter-resolution field is closer to what is needed.

**3. "Quench is true to the engine as shipped" (C). Confirmed, with one gap.**
- **What I checked:** `wiki/liquids-and-gases.md`.
- **What it confirms:** under a roof, steam drips back exactly where it condensed, and lava crust crumbles to rubble that goes to the bottom. So C's rubble that sinks into a dam footing under the lid matches the engine.
- **D's Quench is the opposite:** it needs the crust to stay put as a wall, which means changing a shipped material. Prefer C's.
- **The gap:** the wiki defines a roof as "anything with ground above it". Whether a slab the player places counts as that ground is unverified, and the Lid card depends on it.

**Unchecked, but too optimistic on their face.**
- **Floodgate: "boil the lake and a minute later it rains on the town, downwind".** Steam under open sky does come back as rain. But nothing I saw says *where* or *how soon*, and the aha depends on the rain landing somewhere the player can tie to their click.
- **Mould's timing.** "Bends within two pulses" has to coexist with a network that thickens, prunes and forms loops inside a four-minute dish. Real slime mould takes hours to do that. A heavy time-lapse will make the 2-second beat a strobe, so settle the speed before designing cards on the beat.
- **"All four explorers invented a lightning game independently."** That is weak evidence that lightning is the best idea, because four agents with the same brief share the same priors. Arc earns first place for how it feels in the hand, not because they agreed.
- **Legibility at phone size.** Several sample levels depend on details 1–3 cells wide: a Fuse marker, a copper smear, a wet streak, a 3-cell wall. At 512x320 on a phone none of those can be seen. Build the phone level size into the first prototype.

## On the coordinator's order

I agree Arc should come first, but build its 2-day hold-and-release probe before the 4-day field. Mould second is right for the owner's interest, but only as the cheap agent prototype with the flashlight. The version that waits on the beat is the one most likely to be watched rather than played. If either probe stalls, Firedamp's burn band is a 2-day test with the clearest aha in the set.
