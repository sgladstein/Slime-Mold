# Review 3: synthesis (2026-11-07)

*Reviewer 3 of 3. My emphasis was finding the game nobody wrote: a merge, or one world where several of these mechanics live together.*

## 1. Top 5

1. **Seep** (new: Firedamp + Mould). A mine is leaking gas, and you grow a slime mould that eats it. The catch is that the mould thins a safe pocket into a burnable one as it eats.
2. **Arc, consolidated.** One lightning game made from the best parts of all four explorers' versions: B's drifting ion trail, D's copper farm and the run around it, A's steam and brine conductors, and C's glass scars.
3. **Mould, with a run.** B's Mould, scored with Lode's "length times loops" multiplier, with dye that shows the flow and real lightning as the final storm.
4. **Firedamp** (A's version, with D's run). This is Seep without the mould, and the fallback if the mould part fails its test.
5. **Quench** (C's version). It ranks here as the cheapest honest test of whether "cards plus this engine's chemistry" is fun, not as the game itself.

## 2. Combinations

**Should flows, gas diffusion, growing networks and mould be the layers of one world? Yes, with one rule.** In any one level the player should *push* one layer and *grow* one layer, and the other layers should only answer. If all four are under the player's hand at once, nobody can tell which click caused which outcome, and that is the satchel's "I have no idea what's going on" again. Seep, below, is that world: you push the air, you grow the mould, and the gas and the fire answer.

**A. Firedamp + Mould = Seep. Strong. My top pick, written up in full in section 4.**
- **What moves where:** Firedamp brings the leaking gas, the burn band and the fast verbs (blow, spark). Mould brings the growing network, its memory and its life cycle.
- **The key change:** the mould's food stops being a smell. The mould *eats the gas itself*, which is real (some microbes live on methane). The gradient it climbs is now physical diffusion, the owner's actual interest, not a scent signal.
- **What each half gains:**
  - Mould's biggest risk was turning into watching. Firedamp's fast threat gives it something to do every few seconds.
  - Firedamp's biggest gap was that nothing lasts: you vent gas and it is gone. The mould is a build that lasts.

**B. The four Arcs, merged into one. Strong. My number 2.**
- A, B and D all invented the same lightning game, and C's Fulgurite is its cousin, so they should be one game.
- **Core from B:** the ion haze that drifts on the wind, and the "Again" bolt that follows it. It is the best surprise any explorer found.
- **Run from D:** one town for the whole run, a copper network that grows, "On strike", and storms that get bigger.
- **Conductors from A:** steam plumes that conduct, brine, and the bolt that boils a lake into a second plume.
- **The lasting mark, from C:** every bolt that passes through sand or soil leaves a glass scar. Glass insulates, so your old strikes become walls that later bolts must go round.

The result has two memories: ions that last about three seconds, and scars that last the whole level. That is the network the owner wants, grown by the storm itself.

**C. Mould + Lode's scoring + Tincture's dye + Arc's bolt. Good grafts, not a merge.**
- **Lode** has the best run structure and the weakest reason to be on this engine. Mould is the opposite. So give Mould Lode's score (tube length times loops closed) and its "lenses", permanent rules picked between dishes, such as "loops through damp ground count double".
- **Tincture's dye** becomes a readability tool. Drop a colour into a tube, watch it ride the pulse, and you *see* which tube is the trunk. That answers "why did that branch die?" without an overlay.
- **Arc's bolt** replaces Mould's end-of-level "three random cuts". Thick, wet tubes conduct, so lightning picks the trunk. A tree-shaped network loses half the dish, and one with loops shrugs it off. The toughness test becomes something you watch happen for a reason, not a dice roll.

**D. Arc + Firedamp: a storm over a gas marsh. Weak, and I would not build it.** Lightning that only lights gas in the burnable band sounds great in one sentence. In play it stacks the two hardest-to-read rules in the whole set: where will the bolt go, and will this gas burn? Each needs its own colour code, and two colour codes on one screen at phone size is mud. Keep them as separate games and let each teach its one rule.

**E. Quench + Floodgate + Delta: one volcano-and-flood valley.** A fine merge, but it makes a level pack, not a new game. All three use the same shipped water, lava and stone, and the merge adds no new verb.

**F. Saltworks + Brine: one crystal game.** They are the same idea twice. Merging them does not fix the slowness they share.

**G. Etch's rust as Mould's rival. No.** It puts two hard-to-predict growths against each other, so the player cannot tell their own pattern from the rust's. B's mycelium fairy ring is a clearer rival.

**H. High Tide + anything. No.** It is the most instantly understood concept in the set. Every graft I tried made it harder to explain without making it more fun.

## 3. Why these five

**1. Seep.** Seep is two games that each fix the other's worst flaw. The gas gives you something urgent to do every ten seconds: blow a pocket away from a lamp, spark a fringe. Meanwhile the mould is your slow investment, a living network that sits on a leak and eats it at the source, so the level moves from firefighting to gardening. The best part is a surprise built into the rules. A pocket too thick to burn is safe. Your mould eats it, which thins it, which makes it burnable. **The garden makes its own bomb**, and nobody has to write that as a rule. It covers all four of the owner's interests in one screen, with each one doing a job. **Biggest risk:** players ignore the mould, because blowing and sparking handle the gas well enough. If so, the merge has failed, and Firedamp alone is the honest result.

**2. Arc, consolidated.** Arc resolves in under a second, loud and branching every time, which is the satchel's missing ingredient. B's trick (a wasted bushy bolt leaves ions, the wind carries them under an overhang, and "Again" follows them) is the best single "nobody wrote that" moment in all 21 concepts. The glass scars mean a level has a history. **Biggest risk:** the bolt feels like rolling dice. All four explorers named this risk, and it is real.

**3. Mould, with a run.** It is the most charming idea in the set, and the owner asked for it. Its three stars (reach, thrift, toughness) are a real graded outcome, and Lode's loop multiplier gives a run its "it went absurd" moment. **Biggest risk:** it is slow, so you watch rather than play. Seep exists to fix that.

**4. Firedamp.** A beginner learns its one rule from a single fizzle ("too thick won't burn, thin it first"), and the threat never stops. **Biggest risk:** the burn band is invisible logic, and nothing you do lasts.

**5. Quench.** It needs almost no new simulation, and the lid-and-drip "quench engine" is a combo the shipped engine already produces. **Biggest risk:** it is slow, and the action happens under a rock where you can't see it.

## 4. Extensions

### 4a. Seep, written up in full

**Pitch.** A mine is leaking gas toward the miners' lamps. You blow it, spark it, and grow a slime mould that eats it. But a mould that eats a thick pocket turns it burnable.

**The first 60 seconds.**
- **0–10 s.** A cross-section of two galleries. A small yellow blob of mould pulses in a damp sump at the bottom left. A crack in the right-hand floor starts to breathe faint blue haze, which rises and gathers under a dome in the roof. Text at the top: "Keep gas off the lamps."
- **10–25 s.** The dome fills and darkens to deep purple. You drag Damp along the floor from the sump toward the crack, and the rock darkens. The mould notices within one pulse: a fan of yellow runs along the wet strip.
- **25–40 s.** Gas spills from the dome up a slanted shaft toward the upper lamp, glowing pink, which means burnable. You hold Blow along the upper roof, and the pink streams left toward the open air shaft. It is the first fast save.
- **40–60 s.** The mould reaches the crack and spreads over it, and a thick bright tube forms back to the sump. The leak's haze visibly shrinks: the mould is eating it at the source. Then the dome's underside turns from purple to pink, because the mould below has been eating it. The text changes: "Your mould is thinning the dome. It can burn now."

**The verb: no cards in the level.** You have a thumb bar of four tools, each with a meter that refills, plus two placeable Fuses.
- **Blow** (hold and drag): a draught along your drag. The haze visibly streams.
- **Spark** (tap): a point flame.
- **Damp** (drag): wets rock. The mould runs fast on wet rock, and flame cannot cross it.
- **Cut** (swipe): snips a tube.
- **Fuse** (place): "goes off when the gas here can burn".

**Why no cards.** The mould already supplies the surprise of "you act and the world answers", and the threat is real-time. A shuffled hand would add a third source of randomness, and card text on a small screen is where the satchel's jargon crept in. The meters do card draw's job: a random tapper runs dry when it matters.

**Cards come back between levels**, as Noita-style perks, pick one of three. Examples: "gas is heavier than air" (easy feeding, but deadly for low lamps), "thick tubes conduct", or "spores ride the draught".

**The rules that make the layers talk.** Each is one sentence, and each is visible.
- Gas rises and spreads. It is drawn in three fixed colours: pale blue is too thin to burn, pink is burnable, and deep purple is too thick.
- The mould grows along rock surfaces, so it can climb walls. Its tips climb toward richer gas. It is fast on damp rock and slow on dry.
- Where the mould touches gas, it eats it. That food flows down the tubes to the core, and tubes that carry flow get thick and bright, while idle tubes fade and pull back (B's Physarum rule).
- **Fed tubes are wet, and flame cannot cross them. Starving tubes dry out and burn like a fuse.** The health of the network decides how fire moves through it.
- A tube sitting on a crack "caps" it, eating the leak as it comes out.

**One surprising chain.** The upper gallery holds a deep-purple roof pocket, too thick to burn. Lamp A sits further along the same roof.
1. You Damp a strip of wall below the pocket. Water darkens the rock and drips (shipped liquids).
2. The mould races up the wet strip in about three seconds and fans out across the pocket's underside. It has found food, so the tubes thicken.
3. Eating thins the pocket from below. Its lower skin turns pink: the safe pocket now has a burnable skin, and the gallery's draught is carrying that skin toward lamp A. **Your garden made the danger.**
4. You Spark the skin at the far end from the lamp. A flame runs across the pink layer only, because the purple core above still won't burn.
5. The flame reaches the mould. The thick, fed trunk is wet and holds as a firebreak. The thin fan tips burn and curl back. You lose the fringe, not the network.
6. With the skin gone, the mould eats the next layer down, and you repeat. The pocket is "breathed away" in pulses, by eating and burning in turn. If the core was big enough, one pulse hands over to the shipped explosion and cracks the roof into the gallery above. That is a bang you never aimed, and now that gallery has a gas problem.
7. When the pocket is gone, the tubes up there carry nothing, so they fade and pull back on their own, and the mould's reserve goes up. Nobody wrote "retreat after the job is done".

**What makes doing nothing lose.** The mould starts in a damp sump far from the crack. Left alone, it spreads as a flat sheet over the damp floor it is already on: wide, wasteful, and nowhere near the roof where the gas collects. The gas reaches lamp A in about 45 seconds. **Random play loses too:** Blow pushes pink gas onto a lamp, Spark in purple does nothing, a careless Cut starves the trunk so it dries and becomes a fuse, and Damp in the wrong place leads the mould away from the gas.

**A graded outcome.**
- **The score:** carts hauled from each face. A face goes dark while burnable gas is near its lamp. A small singe costs it 10 seconds. A full blow costs the face for good.
- **1 star:** survive with one face working.
- **2 stars:** every face survives.
- **3 stars:** every crack capped by mould by the end. The mine "breathes": the garden is handling it.
- **Carried forward:** when the level ends, the mould fruits, and its stalk count becomes the next level's starting reserve.

The middles are common: you kept both faces but the mould capped only one crack, or you capped everything but blew the low face early.

**A sample level, "Two Lamps"** (256x160).
- **Lower gallery:** y 110–140, x 20–230. The mould blob starts in a damp sump at x 30 with reserve 100. Crack 1 is in the floor at x 180. Above it is a roof dome, x 160–200, y 95–110, which fills first. Lamp B is low, at x 100, y 125. Rising gas leaves it alone unless someone Blows it down.
- **Shaft:** a slanted shaft climbs from x 200, y 110 to the upper gallery.
- **Upper gallery:** y 40–65, x 20–240. Its roof rises to the right. Lamp A is at the high end, x 235, y 45, which is where overflowing gas pools. An open air shaft at x 20–30 reaches the surface, and gas blown into it escapes.
- **Water:** a natural drip runs down the rock at x 150 between the galleries. It is a damp line the mould can find without you.
- **Escalation:** crack 2 opens in the upper gallery floor at 90 seconds.
- **Tools:** Blow, Spark, Damp, Cut, and 2 Fuses. Goal: 30 carts in 4 minutes.
- **Intended solve:**
  1. Blow the first overflow left to the air shaft, to buy time.
  2. Damp a floor path from the sump to crack 1.
  3. Let the mould thin the dome, and Spark its skin while the draught runs away from lamp B.
  4. Put a Fuse at the top of the shaft for any overflow.
  5. Lead the mould up the x 150 drip in time to meet crack 2.

**The smallest prototype (6 days, in stages, with a stop rule).**
- **Days 1–2:** the gas on the fine grid (it rises, spreads and escapes at the air shaft) with the three-colour ramp, one crack, and Blow. Blow pushes the gas directly rather than through the air grid (see section 5, claim 2). Lamps count exposure.
- **Day 3:** Spark and the burn band. A front runs through pink cells and stops at blue, purple or wet. A big pocket hands over to the shipped explosion.
- **Days 4–5:** the mould, **built as a graph from the start** (junction nodes, with tubes as edges drawn into cells), not as cells turned into a graph later. Tips climb the gas, rock-surface growth, eating, thicken and prune by flow.
- **Day 6:** Damp, Cut, the wet-and-dry tube rule, and "Two Lamps".
- **Publish one page:** a paired run on the same seed, doing nothing against a planned run. Beside each picture put lamps blown, face-seconds worked, cracks capped, and gas eaten by mould against gas burnt, plus the `ascii` worst frame during a burn.
- **Stop rule:** if, in three fresh playtests, players never Damp or Cut after the first minute, the mould is decoration. Ship Firedamp alone, using days 1–3 as built.

### 4b. Arc, consolidated

- **Keep the cards here, unlike Seep.** Arc's combos are about order ("Bushy, then Again"; "On strike, then Fork, then Wire") and each resolves in under a second, which is exactly where Noita's grammar pays off. Use a short row of 3 or 4 slots, not a hand, so the next cast is always the outlined one.
- **A new verb: charge and release.** Hold to pull charge into the cloud above your thumb, and release to strike. The longer you hold, the heavier the bolt. It turns "the charge bar fills" from a timer you wait on into a decision you make.
- **A new surprise from the scars:** glass insulates, so a level fills up with your own failed strikes, and late bolts are funnelled through the gaps between them. Your misses become the maze.
- **Phone reading:** draw the *likeliest* path as a faint dotted line before each cast (B's preview), and after the strike keep the real bolt's branches lit for two seconds beside it. Seeing the gap between "likely" and "actual" is how players learn what moves a bolt.
- **A sharper first probe (1 day):** skip the charge solve. Before each strike, work out one field: how far each cell is from the ground, measured through conductors. Grow the bolt down that field with randomness, lay ions behind it, and let the wind drift them. Add only Bushy and Again, and one lamp under an overhang. If the GIF of "a wasted bolt, then Again hooks under the overhang" doesn't make people grin, the rest of Arc won't either.

### 4c. Mould, with a run

- **Cast on the beat.** A card or tool used *on* the pulse travels the network at once, and one used off the beat waits for the next pulse. That is a rhythm verb, and it makes a planned player visibly faster than a random one.
- **Dye** (from Tincture): show the flow at a glance (see C above).
- **The finale is lightning** (from Arc): see C above.
- **A sharper prototype:** use Seep's mould code (4a, days 4–5) with scattered oats in place of the gas. Mould and Seep should share one mould, not have two.

## 5. Wrong or optimistic claims

I checked three things in the engine source.

**1. "Today's lightning is drawing only." Checked: true.** In `src/sim/weather.rs`, `strike()` is a pure function that returns a position and a brightness. Its only caller, `World::lightning_at` in `src/sim/world.rs`, is read only from `src/render.rs`. So every Arc's bolt, charge and hit effects are entirely new work. B and D said so honestly.

**2. "The field machinery" is coarser than several concepts assume. Checked: the shared air-and-heat grid in `src/sim/field.rs` has `FIELD_SCALE = 16`.**
- That makes one field cell 16x16 world cells, so the 512x320 world is a 32x20 grid.
- Wind carries temperature, light and moisture on that grid. I found no wind or drift in the separate pheromone field (`src/sim/pheromone.rs`).
- What this means:
  - **B's ion haze** "drifting with the existing weather wind" needs new work. Either it lives on the 16-cell grid, which is far too blocky for a trail one bolt wide, or a fine field gets wind added to it.
  - **D's Firedamp** gas "using the shipped field machinery" needs both rising *and* wind added, not just rising.
  - **A's Puff and Draw**, as pushes on the air grid, cannot resolve a gallery narrower than about 16 cells. Mine levels need wide galleries, or the tools should push the gas directly, as I propose for Seep.
  - **Tincture's** idea of running the air solver over a 40-cell-deep river gives a current about 2.5 field cells deep. That is too coarse to carry dye in ribbons.

**3. "Burning warms the air, which rises and draws fresh air in" (A's Firedamp combo, step 4, which A itself marked "to check"), and the "Warm" chimney card. I found no sign that heat pushes the air.** In `src/sim/field.rs`, temperature is carried by the wind, but nothing I found turns heat into pressure or wind. The "buoyancy" in `src/sim/update.rs` is hot gas *cells* (smoke, steam) rising, not warm rock making a draught. I searched for this rather than reading the whole solver, so treat it as "new until shown otherwise". A's breathing-fire combo depends on it.

**Judged optimistic, not checked:**
- **Mould in 4–5 days.** Solving flow on a junction graph while the network grows cell by cell means re-deriving the graph from the cells every few frames, and that alone could eat the prototype. Build the graph first and draw the cells from it (4a).
- **Fault's** hand-off from a closed crack loop to the rigid-body code, in 4 days. By `CLAUDE.md`'s own record, structural collapse is the subsystem whose playtests overturned three models that all looked correct in tests.
- **"Every card resolves within 3 seconds"** for Saltworks, Brine and Delta. Evaporation, crystals and erosion each take minutes. Speeding them up enough also changes how they look.
- **Arc being "the cheapest to run"** (B). The *field* is cheap if you solve it only on a cast. But every hit that boils, lights or blasts wakes chunks across the bolt's whole path, and a storm of six quick bolts is the worst-frame case. Measure it during a cast, as B itself asks.
